Exercise System

Astronauts exercise about two hours every day to protect muscles, bones, and cardiovascular fitness.

Treadmill (T2)

A harness loads the body so running feels more like Earth. This helps maintain bone density and leg strength. Vibration isolation keeps the exercise from shaking the station.

Cycle Ergometer (CEVIS)

Used for aerobic conditioning. Astronauts pedal while restrained so they stay in place. Protocols are based on pre-flight fitness tests.

Advanced Resistive Exercise Device (ARED)

Provides resistance training that mimics free weights. Crew members perform squats, deadlifts, rows, and other moves to protect muscles and bones.

Daily Routine

Typical plan mixes resistance and aerobic work six days a week. Exercise is the primary countermeasure against the “use it or lose it” effects of microgravity.

Sleep & Nutrition

Sleep

Each crew member has a small private quarter. A sleeping bag is tethered so the person does not float away. Eye masks and earplugs help block light and constant fan noise. Lighting is adjusted to support circadian rhythms even with 16 sunrises a day.

Nutrition

Meals are carefully planned to supply enough calories, protein, calcium, and vitamin D. Proper nutrition supports bone health, muscle repair, and overall energy. Water is recycled and monitored closely.

Why It Matters

Without good sleep and nutrition, the benefits of exercise are reduced. Consistent routines also help crew members manage the stress of a confined, high-workload environment.

Medical Care Station

Onboard Monitoring

Crew members regularly check vital signs, perform ultrasound scans, and complete health questionnaires. Data is reviewed by flight surgeons on the ground.

Medical Supplies

The station carries a pharmacy of medications, bandages, IV supplies, airway tools, and a defibrillator. Ultrasound is the main imaging tool because there is no X-ray or MRI.

Remote Guidance

There is usually no physician on board. A trained crew medical officer works with flight surgeons on Earth via secure video links for diagnosis and treatment decisions.

Emergency Care

Procedures exist for serious illness or injury. In low Earth orbit, a crew member can be returned to Earth if needed. Future deep-space missions will require more autonomous care.

Mental Well-Being Space

Isolation, confinement, and distance from family create unique challenges. Habitat design and daily practices help protect mental health.

Privacy

Private sleep quarters give each person a place to rest and store personal items. This small personal space is important for long missions.

Routines & Recreation

Regular schedules, exercise, movies, music, reading, and looking at Earth from the windows help mark time and reduce stress.

Communication

Scheduled video calls and messages with family and friends are among the strongest supports for crew morale.

Crew Support

Shared meals, group activities, and open communication among the crew build cohesion and help people manage difficult days.

Radiation Protection

Habitat Shielding

The station’s structure and equipment provide some protection from galactic cosmic rays and trapped particles. In low Earth orbit the geomagnetic field also helps. Personal dosimeters track each crew member’s exposure.

Monitoring

Radiation sensors inside the modules continuously measure dose rates. Ground teams watch solar activity and can warn the crew of approaching solar particle events.

Solar Events

During a strong solar particle event, crew members can move to better-shielded areas and rearrange water containers or other mass for extra protection. Procedures are practiced before flight.

Limits

Career dose limits help protect long-term health. Future missions beyond Earth’s magnetic field will need stronger shielding and better forecasting.

Established Practices vs Future Concepts

Current (ISS & Near-Term)

  • ~2 hours daily exercise (ARED, treadmill, cycle)
  • Private sleep quarters and circadian lighting
  • Telemedicine with flight surgeons on Earth
  • Onboard pharmacy, ultrasound, and emergency kits
  • Personal dosimeters and solar-event procedures
  • Regular health monitoring and nutrition planning

Future Concepts

  • More autonomous medical systems for deep space
  • Improved exercise hardware with less time and mass
  • Better radiation shelters and wearable shielding
  • Artificial gravity or hybrid countermeasures
  • Enhanced mental-health tools for multi-year missions
  • Still based on proven exercise, monitoring, and crew support

Future habitats will need to keep crews healthy for months or years with limited or delayed support from Earth, while building on the systems that already work on the International Space Station.
